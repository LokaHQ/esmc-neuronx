Introduction #

Protein variant effect prediction is one of the most consequential tasks in computational biology. Every human protein can be altered by thousands of single-amino-acid substitutions. Some cause disease. Most do not. Distinguishing the two, at scale, is the foundation of therapeutic target selection, clinical variant interpretation, and personalised medicine pipelines.

The traditional approach is expensive and slow: wet-lab deep mutational scanning, computational homology modelling, or population-scale gnomAD lookups. Protein language models changed the economics. A model trained on hundreds of millions of natural protein sequences learns, implicitly, which amino acids are tolerated at each position and which are not. A substitution that is unlikely under the model's learned distribution is likely pathogenic; one close to wild type in log-probability space is likely benign.

ESMC-300M, from EvolutionaryScale, is a strong open option for this task. It is an encoder-only model of the ESM-C family, trained on sequences from UniRef and MGnify. The weights are public, the architecture is a standard bidirectional Transformer, and zero-shot variant scoring needs no fine-tuning.

The practical question for teams building on AWS is the same we keep hearing: can we take this open checkpoint and run it on Trainium2 without turning the first experiment into a kernel engineering project? The answer is yes. ESMC-300M compiled and ran on a single trn2.3xlarge through torch.compile(backend="neuron"), with one practical fix—disabling Flash Attention.

We evaluated on D2Deep: 125,190 human protein variants (109,700 benign, 15,490 pathogenic), scored zero-shot using the log-probability delta between the mutant and wild-type amino acid in context. The estimated 4×NeuronCore batch=16 configuration reaches ~407 samples/s at ~$1.52 per million samples scored—1.7× lower cost than H100 at comparable throughput.

Closed beta: The Native PyTorch integration for Trainium used here—TorchNeuron—is currently a closed (private) beta, available only by request through an AWS Neuron support representative, and is not yet generally available. Per AWS, Neuron transitions from PyTorch/XLA to native PyTorch via TorchNeuron starting with PyTorch 2.10. Treat this as an early look at where Neuron's PyTorch support is heading.

What makes ESMC different #

Why this section matters: ESMC is encoder-only—one forward pass per variant, no autoregressive loop. That changes everything about how you deploy it and why Trainium2 is a natural fit.

Most protein language model discussions lead with benchmark numbers. The architecture choice matters more for infrastructure teams. ESMC is encoder-only: bidirectional attention over all residues simultaneously, no decode loop, no KV (Key-Value) cache. For variant scoring, that is exactly what you want. One forward pass per variant: feed in the wild-type sequence, read log-softmax at the mutation position, compute the delta. No generation, no sampling.

The architecture is a standard bidirectional Transformer with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE), SwiGLU feed-forward layers, and a 33-token protein vocabulary. ESMC-300M loads in ~0.6 GB at bfloat16 and runs efficiently at batch=1—the natural shape for single-variant scoring.

The one Neuron compatibility requirement is disabling Flash Attention. ESMC uses Flash Attention by default; passing use_flash_attn=False at load time switches to standard scaled-dot-product attention, which compiles cleanly. Easy to miss and a blocker if overlooked. The esm_neuron package in this repository handles it automatically.

Why Trainium2 #

Protein variant scoring has a specific production shape. It is batch scoring: thousands or millions of variants, processed inside a secure cloud environment where the sequence data cannot leave the VPC. Cost per sample matters. Data locality matters.

This is where Trainium2 fits naturally. A trn2.3xlarge gives one Trainium2 accelerator with 4 NeuronCores and 96 GB of accelerator memory at ~$2.235/hr (Capacity Blocks, checked May 2026). The estimated full D2Deep evaluation—125,190 variants—completes in ~310 seconds. At that rate, one trn2.3xlarge can score roughly 35 million variants per day.

Why Trainium2 matters here: variant scoring is a batch workload. The value is sustained throughput at a predictable cost, inside the AWS environment where the sequence data already lives.

ESMC's encoder architecture maps well onto Trainium2's NeuronCore. Unlike autoregressive generation—where each token depends on the last and the model runs N times per sequence—ESMC does one forward pass per sample with fully static shapes once sequence length is fixed. Static shapes are exactly what the Neuron compiler is designed for.

Benchmark setup #

We ran the primary benchmark on a single trn2.3xlarge (4 NeuronCores, 96 GB accelerator memory). Four Docker workers ran in parallel, each pinned to one NeuronCore via NEURON_RT_VISIBLE_CORES, processing a quarter of the D2Deep dataset independently. We also ran a single-core batch=16 experiment to establish per-core throughput at high batch.

Results and analysis #

The headline result is compatibility and quality. ESMC-300M compiled cleanly on Trainium2 with no model rewrite. Quality is identical to the CUDA baseline: ROC-AUC 0.8525 on Trainium2 versus 0.8524 on H100—a difference of 0.0001 attributable to bfloat16 accumulation.

Full-instance throughput

Each NeuronCore scores at 101.8 samples/s at batch=16. With four NeuronCores in parallel, the estimated full-instance throughput is 4 × 101.8 = ~407 samples/s—within 2% of H100 at batch=16 (414 samples/s).

At batch=1, a single Trainium2 NeuronCore scores ~55 samples/s (220.3 ÷ 4) versus 35.9 samples/s on H100. Trainium2 NeuronCore is 1.53× faster per unit at batch=1. ESMC's 512-token encoder forward pass at batch=1 leaves most of an H100's SMs idle; the NeuronCore's smaller, purpose-built MatMul engine is a better fit for this regime.

H100 batch scaling

On H100, throughput scales 11.5× from batch=1 to batch=16, while p50 latency stays near-flat up to batch=4 (27 ms at batch=1 versus 28 ms at batch=4). Batch=16 is the practical sweet spot at seq_len=512.

Why this matters for bio and HCLS #

Protein variant effect prediction is not a research convenience. It is the engine behind clinical variant interpretation, therapeutic target prioritisation, and personalised medicine pipelines. ClinVar receives millions of variant submissions per year. AlphaMissense scored every human missense variant. The computational infrastructure to do this at population scale, inside a secure AWS environment, is no longer optional for serious bio and HCLS organisations.

ESMC and Trainium2 together close a gap that has frustrated many teams: open model, Trainium2 infrastructure, practical cost. The model is open. The weights are public. The scoring approach needs no fine-tuning and no labelled training data. And now it runs on the same accelerator infrastructure teams already operate in their AWS environment, at a cost that fits a serious production budget.

For teams at pharma companies, diagnostics labs, and clinical AI startups, the relevance is direct. Scoring every variant in a target protein before a clinical trial takes minutes on a trn2.3xlarge. Scoring every missense variant in a proteome of 20,000 proteins takes hours, not weeks. The bottleneck shifts from infrastructure to biology.

Where Loka fits: taking strong open protein models, making them run on AWS infrastructure, measuring them honestly, and turning the results into something a CTO can evaluate with procurement and security in the room. This benchmark is exactly that.

Conclusion #

The core result is straightforward: one argument change at load time (use_flash_attn=False), regional compile, and four parallel workers—and ESMC-300M runs on Trainium2.

The numbers tell a clear story. At batch=1, Trainium2's NeuronCore is 1.53× faster than H100 per unit. Scaling to 4×batch=16, each NeuronCore contributes 101.8 s/s, putting the full instance at ~407 s/s: within 2% of H100 at batch=16, at ~1.7× lower cost per million samples scored ($1.52 vs $2.64).

The bottleneck for protein AI is increasingly the gap between an open model release and a production-grade scoring pipeline inside a regulated AWS environment. With ESMC-300M, Trainium2, and torch.compile, that gap is now a matter of hours, not months.
