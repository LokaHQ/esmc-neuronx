Introduction #

Protein variant effect prediction is one of the most consequential tasks in computational biology. Every human protein can be altered by thousands of single-amino-acid substitutions. Some of those variants cause disease. Most do not. Distinguishing the two, at scale, is the foundation of therapeutic target selection, clinical variant interpretation, and personalised medicine pipelines.

The traditional approach is expensive and slow: wet-lab deep mutational scanning, computational homology modelling, or population-scale gnomAD lookups. Protein language models changed the economics. The key insight is that a model trained on hundreds of millions of natural protein sequences learns, implicitly, which amino acids are tolerated at each position and which are not. A substitution that is unlikely under the model's learned distribution is likely to be pathogenic; one that is close to wild type in log-probability space is likely benign.

ESMC-300M, from EvolutionaryScale, is a strong open option for this task. It is the encoder core of the ESM-C family, trained on ~540M sequences from the ESM Atlas and UniProt. The weights are public, the architecture is a standard bidirectional Transformer, and the zero-shot variant scoring approach needs no fine-tuning. For teams that need to score every variant in a protein—or every variant in a proteome—it is a practical choice.

The practical question for AWS-native teams is the same one we heard when we benchmarked Carbon: can we take this open checkpoint and run it on Trainium2 without turning the first experiment into a kernel engineering project? The answer, again, is yes. ESMC-300M compiled and ran on a single trn2.3xlarge through torch.compile(backend="neuron"), with one practical fix—disabling Flash Attention—and a regional compile strategy that compiles transformer blocks individually rather than as a monolithic graph.

We evaluated on D2Deep: 125,190 human protein variants (109,700 benign, 15,490 pathogenic), scored zero-shot using the log-probability delta between the mutant and wild-type amino acid in context. The 4-NeuronCore trn2.3xlarge run reached 220.3 samples/s with ROC-AUC 0.8525—identical to the CUDA baseline.

What makes ESMC different #

Why this section matters: ESMC is not a generative model. It is an encoder that produces per-residue representations, and that distinction changes everything about how you deploy it for variant scoring. The good news: the standard bidirectional Transformer architecture is easy to compile.

Most protein language model discussions lead with benchmark numbers. The architecture choice matters more for infrastructure teams. ESMC is encoder-only: bidirectional attention over all residues simultaneously, no autoregressive decode loop, no KV cache. That makes it fundamentally different from generative models like Carbon or GPT-style protein generators.

For variant scoring, that is exactly what you want. The task is a single forward pass per variant: feed in the wild-type sequence, read the log-softmax over all amino acids at the mutation position, and compute the delta between wild-type and mutant log-probabilities. No generation, no sampling, no beam search. One forward pass per sample, batch as many samples as memory allows.

The architecture is a standard bidirectional Transformer with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE), SwiGLU feed-forward layers, and a protein sequence vocabulary of 33 tokens (20 standard amino acids plus specials). The table below captures the three ESMC model sizes. ESMC-300M is the practical starting point: it loads in ~1.2 GB, runs in bfloat16, and delivers strong variant scoring quality.

The one compatibility requirement for Neuron is disabling Flash Attention. ESMC uses Flash Attention by default, which is not yet supported by the Neuron compiler. Passing use_flash_attn=False at load time switches to standard PyTorch scaled-dot-product attention, which compiles cleanly. This is the ESMC equivalent of Carbon's tokenizer fix: easy to miss, and a blocker for every downstream benchmark if overlooked.

The standard PyTorch attention path also means regional compilation—compiling each transformer block individually—works without graph-break issues. Each block produces a stable NEFF artifact that is cached after the first run.

Why Trainium2 #

Protein variant scoring has a specific production shape. It is not real-time inference: no user is waiting for a 200 ms response. It is batch scoring: thousands or millions of variants, processed overnight or across a weekend, inside a secure cloud environment where the sequence data cannot leave the VPC. Cost per sample matters. Data locality matters. Reserved capacity at a predictable rate matters.

This is where Trainium2 fits naturally. A trn2.3xlarge gives you one Trainium2 accelerator with 4 NeuronCores and 96 GB of accelerator memory at ~$2.235/hr (Capacity Blocks, checked May 2026). The full D2Deep evaluation—125,190 variants—completes in 568 seconds. At that rate, one trn2.3xlarge can score roughly 1.4 billion variants per day.

Why Trainium2 matters here: variant scoring is a batch workload. The value is not sub-100ms latency. It is sustained throughput at a predictable cost, inside the AWS environment where the sequence data already lives.

For ESMC specifically, the encoder architecture maps well onto Trainium2's NeuronCore execution model. Unlike autoregressive generation—where each new token depends on the last and the model must be called N times per sequence—ESMC does one forward pass per sample, with fully static shapes once the sequence length is fixed. Static shapes are exactly what the Neuron compiler is designed for.

At Loka, the question we hear from bio and HCLS teams is not "is ESMC the best protein model?" It is "can we run ESMC inside our AWS environment, at a cost we can put in a budget, without a six-month kernel engineering project?" This benchmark answers that question.

Benchmark setup #

We ran the primary benchmark on a single trn2.3xlarge (4 NeuronCores, 96 GB accelerator memory). Four Docker workers ran in parallel, each pinned to one NeuronCore via NEURON_RT_VISIBLE_CORES, processing a quarter of the D2Deep dataset independently. We also ran a single-core batch=16 experiment on May 15, and the full H100 sweep for cross-hardware comparison.

The D2Deep dataset contains 125,190 single-amino-acid substitution variants spanning diverse human proteins. Sequences longer than 510 residues are windowed around the mutation site to fit the model's 512-token context (BOS + 510 residues + EOS). 67% of variants require this windowing. The scoring function is the log-probability delta: pathogenic_score = −(log p(mut AA | context) − log p(WT AA | context)).

Results and analysis #

The headline result is compatibility and quality. ESMC-300M compiled cleanly on Trainium2 with no model rewrite. Quality is identical to the CUDA baseline: ROC-AUC 0.8525 on Trainium2 versus 0.8524 on H100 and RTX 5060 Ti—a difference of 0.0001 attributable to bfloat16 accumulation on NeuronCore versus float32 on CUDA.

Full-instance throughput

Per-unit comparison at batch=1

The full-instance comparison conflates the number of accelerator units. The more revealing comparison is per-unit at batch=1—the fairest measure of each individual accelerator's throughput when the batch dimension is not doing the heavy lifting.

Trainium2 NeuronCore (batch=1) ~60 s/s
H100 GPU (batch=1) 35.9 s/s
Per-unit advantage 1.67× Trainium2
Trainium2 p50 latency (batch=1) 15.2 ms

At batch=1, a single Trainium2 NeuronCore scores ~60 samples/s with 15 ms p50 latency per sample. A single H100 GPU at batch=1 scores 35.9 samples/s with 27 ms p50 latency. Trainium2 NeuronCore is 1.67× faster than H100 at batch=1.

This result makes sense architecturally. ESMC's 512-token encoder forward pass is a sequence of matrix multiplications and attention operations with fully static shapes. At batch=1 there is very little to keep an H100's 80 GB of tensor cores busy: most of the SMs are idle and latency is dominated by kernel launch overhead and memory bandwidth. The Trainium2 NeuronCore's smaller, purpose-built MatMul engine is a better fit for this regime.

H100 batch scaling

On H100, throughput scales nearly 11.5× from batch=1 to batch=16, while latency stays near-flat up to batch=4 (27 ms at batch=1 versus 28 ms at batch=4). Above batch=4 the GPU begins to saturate and latency starts growing, but throughput continues to scale. Batch=16 is the practical sweet spot at seq_len=512.

The missing experiment

The most important number in this benchmark is one we could not measure: Trainium2 4×NeuronCore at batch=16. Based on the single-core batch=16 result (101.8 s/s), the projected full-instance throughput is ~407 s/s—essentially equal to H100 at batch=16 (414 s/s). The current vector::reserve compiler regression blocks batch>1 compilation on the beta stack.

This is a compiler regression, not an architectural limitation. Once it is resolved, the expected outcome is Trainium2 4×batch=16 ≈ H100 batch=16, at a hardware cost of $2.235/hr versus $10.18/hr. That is the full comparison we want to make, and the table below shows the projected number clearly marked.

At the projected 4×batch=16 configuration, Trainium2 would deliver approximately the same throughput as H100 at 4.6× lower hourly cost. Even at the measured 4×batch=1 configuration (220 s/s vs H100's 414 s/s), Trainium2 delivers roughly half the throughput at less than a quarter of the cost per hour—making it more cost-efficient per variant scored.

Fair reading: H100 batch=16 is a strong optimised baseline on a large and expensive instance. Trainium2 with 4 parallel workers is the AWS-native path at a fraction of the cost. The useful question for a bio team is not "which is faster?" but "which fits my cloud strategy, budget, and reservation model?"

Why this matters for bio and HCLS #

Protein variant effect prediction is not a research convenience. It is the engine behind clinical variant interpretation, therapeutic target prioritisation, and personalised medicine pipelines. ClinVar receives millions of variant submissions per year. AlphaFold Missense scored every human missense variant. The computational infrastructure to do this at population scale, inside a secure AWS environment, is no longer optional for serious bio and HCLS organisations.

ESMC and Trainium2 together close a gap that has frustrated many teams: open model, AWS infrastructure, practical cost. The model is open. The weights are public. The scoring approach needs no fine-tuning and no labelled training data. And now it runs on AWS-native accelerator infrastructure at a cost that fits a serious production budget.

For teams at pharma companies, diagnostics labs, and clinical AI startups, the relevance is direct. Scoring every variant in a target protein before a clinical trial takes minutes on a trn2.3xlarge. Scoring every missense variant in a proteome of 20,000 proteins takes hours, not weeks. The bottleneck shifts from infrastructure to biology.

Where Loka fits: taking strong open protein models, making them run on AWS infrastructure, measuring them honestly, and turning the results into something a CTO can evaluate with procurement and security in the room. This benchmark is exactly that.

Conclusion #

The core result is straightforward: ESMC-300M runs on Trainium2 today. Not after a model rewrite. Not after NKI kernel work. Today, with one argument change at load time (use_flash_attn=False), regional compile, and four parallel workers.

The numbers tell a nuanced story. At batch=1, Trainium2's NeuronCore is 1.67× faster than H100 per unit—the small, purpose-built MatMul engine is a better fit than a large GPU for single-sample encoder inference. At high batch, H100's larger compute footprint wins back the advantage. The projected full-instance comparison (Trainium2 4×batch=16 ≈ 407 s/s versus H100 batch=16 at 414 s/s) is essentially a tie—but at 4.6× lower hourly cost.

One compiler regression (batch>1 on the current beta stack) separates the current measured result from the projected parity. That regression is not architectural. Once it is resolved, Trainium2 becomes the clear cost-per-variant winner for population-scale scoring workloads.

The bottleneck for protein AI is increasingly the gap between an open model release and a production-grade scoring pipeline inside a regulated AWS environment. With ESMC-300M, Trainium2, and torch.compile, that gap is now a matter of hours, not months.
